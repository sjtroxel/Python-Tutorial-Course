## 1. The verdict

You have built two machines. The first machine turns your time into skills, and it works: eight deployed AI systems, a completed Python course, a rebuilt LinkedIn profile, a four-post technical blog series, a production Python eval microservice. The second machine turns skills into interviews, and it has never been switched on.

Your own plan from May 2026 said: "Begin applying to roles in parallel. Do NOT wait until the project is done. Apply to 5-10 companies per week once active. Job-ready is already achieved by the current portfolio." That was written in May. It is now June 9, and the application count is zero.

This means something important and uncomfortable in both directions:

- **Uncomfortable:** the bottleneck is not a missing skill, framework, or project. No amount of additional learning moves the needle while the count stays at zero. You said this yourself tonight, and you are right.
- **Important:** there is no negative market data about you. Zero applications means zero rejections. The silence you have been reading as a verdict is an unplugged phone. You cannot conclude the market has rejected you before entering the market.

The plan does not change. The Masterclass starts Thursday; the Python dual-loop continues. The only change: applications run in parallel, starting this week, and this document is the manual for how.

## 2. Will this succeed? The market, as of June 2026

Nobody can promise an outcome. What the current data supports:

- Software engineering job listings are up roughly 30% in 2026, the highest demand in over three years.
- Job postings requiring AI skills grew 109% from 2024 to 2026. AI Engineer remains LinkedIn's fastest-growing title.
- Entry-level AI engineering compensation is published at $120K-$150K. The $100K+ target is the floor of the band, not a stretch.
- The hiring bar has reshaped around exactly four questions: Can you find problems independently? Can you connect business context? Can you direct AI tools to build the solution? Can you verify what AI produces? That is a description of how you already work.
- The honest risks: only ~2.5% of AI-titled postings target 0-2 years of experience, and a candidate without employer-verified tech history converts poorly through cold resume screens. These are funnel-shape problems. They dictate strategy (Section 4), not surrender.

On the "is AI moving too fast / will the role disappear" question: postings for pure-implementation roles (translating specs into code) fell 17% over the past year, while postings requiring AI-tooling fluency rose 340%. The disruption is real and you are on the profitable side of it. And because the AI engineering discipline is roughly two years old, the "more experienced competition" mostly is not more experienced *at this* — everyone's AI-engineering tenure is measured in months, and you have shipped more production AI systems than most people applying for the same roles.

## 4. The playbook: how to find postings and apply

Written for someone who has never done this. None of it is mysterious; it is a repetitive clerical process that everyone finds tedious and nobody finds hard once the first rep is done.

### Step 0 — Resume (one session, ~2 hours, do before the first application)

- One page. Derived almost entirely from the LinkedIn profile that already exists: headline, the AI Software Engineer (Self-Directed) experience entry, the Codefi entries, the four project blurbs with live URLs, the skills list.
- Sections: Name/contact/GitHub/LinkedIn → Summary (2 lines) → Projects (4, each with stack and one production-grade detail) → Experience → Education → Skills.
- Projects go ABOVE experience. For a portfolio-first candidate the projects are the experience.
- Format: plain, single-column, no graphics (ATS parsers mangle multi-column layouts). Export to PDF.
- Build it with Claude in one sitting. Do not polish for a week. A 7/10 resume sent beats a 10/10 resume drafted.

### Step 1 — Where postings live (verified June 2026)

| Source | What it is | How to use |
|---|---|---|
| LinkedIn Jobs | Largest volume | Searches + saved alerts (see Step 2). Open-to-Work is already configured from May. |
| [Wellfound](https://wellfound.com/role/r/ai-engineer) | Startup-focused; apply with one profile, often direct to hiring managers | Best fit for the "Series A/B startup" target. Set up profile once, then apply natively. |
| Hacker News "Who's Hiring" | Monthly thread, posted the 1st of each month | Search the thread for "AI", "LLM", "RAG", "remote". Postings are written by engineers and often skip the ATS entirely — email a human directly. |
| [Built In](https://builtin.com/jobs/remote/dev-engineering/search/artificial-intelligence-engineer) | Tech-hub job board with remote section | Also useful for the Kansas City target via location filters. |
| [Remotive](https://remotive.com) / [Himalayas](https://himalayas.app/) | Vetted remote boards with AI sections | Secondary; check weekly. |
| aijobs.net / AI-specific boards | AI-only listings | Secondary; volume is lower but signal is high. |
| Company careers pages | For companies you already admire | Anthropic customer companies, AI-product startups you read about. Direct applications skip aggregator competition. |

### Step 2 — Search terms and filters

Run these as saved searches with email alerts (LinkedIn: Jobs → search → toggle "Set alert"):

- "AI Engineer" — remote, United States
- "Generative AI Engineer" — remote
- "Backend Engineer" + TypeScript or Node.js — remote (this is the wide funnel; companies adding AI features to Node backends are hiring exactly your stack)
- "Full Stack Engineer" + AI or LLM — remote
- Any of the above filtered to Kansas City, MO (the remote-with-occasional-in-person target)

**The widest funnel matters most.** Do not only apply to AI-titled roles (the 2.5% junior problem). "Backend engineer who builds AI features" was always the most likely first role, and in that pool the portfolio is a differentiator instead of a minimum.

### Step 3 — How to read a posting

- The requirements list is a wish list, not a checklist. Companies write them for an imaginary candidate. **Apply at roughly 50-60% match.** Everyone qualified applies underqualified on paper.
- "3-5 years experience" means "we want someone who can ship without hand-holding." Eight deployed systems is an argument for that. Let them say no; do not say no for them.
- Hard skip only on: security clearance requirements, on-site-only in distant cities, and stacks with zero overlap (pure embedded C, pure mobile native).
- Ignore salary ranges that start below $90K; the band data says you do not need to.

### Step 4 — Applying (what actually happens when you click the button)

- **LinkedIn Easy Apply:** resume upload + 3-5 form questions. Under 10 minutes.
- **ATS forms (Greenhouse, Lever, Ashby):** resume upload + fields the parser pre-fills + occasionally "why us?" Keep a snippets file with reusable answers: a 3-sentence intro, a paragraph on Wildlife Sentinel, a paragraph on Heritage Odyssey's eval service, links block. Assembling from snippets takes 15 minutes per application.
- **Cover letters:** skip unless the company is small and genuinely interesting. A short, specific note ("I built a production multi-agent system that does X; your product does adjacent-X") beats a formal letter.
- **Tracking:** one spreadsheet or markdown table. Columns: date, company, role, source, link, status, notes. This is also the anxiety instrument: the goal each week is a number on this sheet, not an outcome.

### Step 5 — Calibrate expectations (read this before week 2)

- Cold application response rates run roughly 2-5%. **Twenty applications producing one screen is normal arithmetic, not a referendum.** Silence is the modal outcome of every individual application and means nothing about any one of them.
- Referral and warm-contact applications convert several times better, which is why Step 6 exists.
- Median time from active search to offer is 3-6 months. The clock starts at application #1, not at readiness. Every deferred week moves the offer date, not the readiness date.

### Step 6 — The human channel (runs alongside, higher conversion)

1. **Post SoilProve on LinkedIn.** The draft already exists from May 26. The bar is "did the post happen," not "did the post win."
2. **The AI Collective KC reach-out.** One message. It is also the lever on the Kansas City remote-with-occasional-in-person target.
3. **Comment meaningfully on 2-3 AI engineering posts per week.** Costs minutes, compounds visibility, and is the lowest-stakes social rep available.
4. When applying to a company, check for any 1st/2nd-degree connection and send one short note. "I just applied for X; I build production multi-agent systems in TypeScript and Python; would you be open to passing my name along?" Worst case is silence, which is where the application already was.

### Step 7 — Interview prep (the parallel workstream, finally started)

- **Format reality:** recruiter screen (15-30 min, "tell me about yourself") → technical conversation or take-home → system design → behavioral. Portfolio-walkthrough, system-design, and take-home formats are strengths. Depth-drilling algorithm formats are the weakness; deprioritize companies that lead with them.
- **The articulation gap is the real risk and it is fixable in weeks.** The rep: for each Masterclass weekly project, write the README/explanation yourself first-draft, then spend 30-60 minutes walking through your own code out loud as if an interviewer asked "how does this work?" Same skill, same muscle.
- **The honest-gap answer template** (already drafted in May, now practice it): "Honest answer: I know X does Y. I used it because Z. The deeper internals are a gap I haven't closed; if I needed to, I'd start with [resource]." Interviewers respect this far more than bluffing, and it converts the articulation anxiety into a script.
- **Tell me about yourself, 60-second version, memorized:** career-switcher who went deep on AI engineering → 8 deployed production AI systems → strongest two in one sentence each → looking for a team shipping production LLM systems. Done.

## 6. On the anxiety, briefly

The application anxiety is real and it is also a known, well-mapped problem with a known shape: the first rep costs the most, every subsequent rep costs less, and batching plus scripts plus a count-based scoreboard strips most of the decision-fatigue out of it. That is why every step above is designed to be small, scripted, and measured by "did it happen" rather than "did it work." You have already proven the pattern on harder things: the LinkedIn rebuild was flagged as the anxiety item in May and it shipped in four days once it became a checklist. This is the same move.

One sentence to keep: **the scariest interpretation of the evidence is not "the market is too hard." It is "I was ready in May."** The fix for that one is cheap, and it is Step 5 of week one.
